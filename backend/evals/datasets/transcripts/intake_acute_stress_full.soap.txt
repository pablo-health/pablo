SUBJECTIVE:
Client is a 35-year-old female presenting for initial evaluation at suggestion of primary care physician, reporting ongoing psychological symptoms following motor vehicle accident (MVA) on February 14, 2024 (approximately 5 months ago). Client describes accident: was driving home from work in heavy rain, another vehicle ran red light and struck client's car on driver's side, causing car to spin and hit pole. Client sustained concussion, bruising, and sprained wrist; other driver sustained broken leg. Client reports she "thought I was fine" initially but has been experiencing increasing awareness of psychological impact over past several months.

Current symptoms reported:
1. Intrusive symptoms: Client reports nightmares about the accident, initially nightly for first month, now decreased to 1-2 times per week. Dreams involve replays of accident with variations (being more seriously injured, unable to escape car) or general themes of driving and impending danger. Client reports intrusive thoughts/memories triggered by reminders (loud noises, seeing accidents on news, certain songs, rain). Describes brief "flashback-like" experiences where she can "kind of see it in my head" with accompanying physical sensations (heart pounding, adrenaline surge), but maintains awareness of present moment and experiences pass "pretty quickly."

2. Avoidance: Client avoids specific intersection where accident occurred (takes "longer routes" to bypass it). Reports working from home more frequently than employer expects (3 days/week in office required, client finding excuses to avoid). Avoids driving at night and in bad weather when possible. Has turned down social invitations that would require driving in these conditions. Reports friends have noticed she is "less available." States she doesn't like talking about accident or how it's affecting her because it "feels like complaining."

3. Negative alterations in cognition/mood: Client reports mood is "up and down" - some days "okay, pretty normal," other days feels "kind of flat, like nothing is that interesting or enjoyable." Increased irritability, particularly regarding driving-related situations (husband's driving, sudden plan changes). Reports feeling "more vulnerable than I used to" and frustrated with self for "still being anxious about it five months later." Self-blame present: "I feel like I should be over it by now... I feel like I'm being weak or overly dramatic." Some sense of changed worldview: "I feel like I can't take anything for granted anymore" and increased awareness that "things can go wrong" quickly. Denies guilt about accident itself (recognizes it was not her fault). Denies anhedonia is pervasive or severe.

4. Alterations in arousal/reactivity: Increased startle response to loud noises (door slams, dropped objects). Self-describes as "hypervigilant" - constantly scanning environment for potential hazards, especially when in vehicles. Concentration difficulties at work (zoning out, requiring rereading, tasks take longer though performance apparently still adequate). Sleep disturbance: difficulty falling asleep with rumination (not always about accident, described as general worry which client notes has "always been kind of a worrier" but "worse since February"). When nightmares occur, unable to return to sleep. Increased anxiety when driving, especially in rain and at intersections (racing heart, sweaty palms). As passenger, experiences heightened vigilance, watching other cars, gasping/tensing (husband has noticed).

Client reports some pre-existing anxiety: "I've always been kind of a worrier... overthink things, worry about what could go wrong. But I managed it okay." States current anxiety feels "different though. More intense. And it's specifically about safety and cars and things that never really bothered me before." Baseline self-rated distress level pre-accident: 2/10. Current distress: varies from 3/10 (good days) to 7-8/10 (when driving in challenging conditions), sitting in session 4/10.

Physical recovery: Concussion symptoms (headaches, dizziness) mostly resolved, though still gets headaches when stressed. Wrist healed. Client reports "physically, I'm pretty much back to normal."

Functional status: Client continues working (though working from home more than expected), marriage described as "solid" though client acknowledges being "more tense and snappy." Still seeing friends less frequently. Has reduced exercise (previously attended gym requiring highway driving, now avoiding). Overall states "I wouldn't say my life has fallen apart or anything. I'm managing. I just don't feel like myself, and I'm tired of feeling anxious all the time."

Coping strategies: Deep breathing, staying busy/distracted, occasionally talking to husband (though protects him from "full extent" to avoid him worrying). Reports these provide some relief.

Denies suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, intent to harm self or others.

OBJECTIVE:
Client presented for video session appearing as stated age, casually dressed, well-groomed. Eye contact was appropriate throughout session. Speech normal rate and volume, coherent and goal-directed. Mood self-described as "stressed and anxious and tired." Affect was somewhat anxious but appropriate to content; demonstrated good range when discussing various topics. No psychomotor agitation or retardation observed. Client was engaged throughout session, answered questions thoughtfully and with detail. Demonstrated good insight into her symptoms ("I think it's because I'm not in control" regarding passenger anxiety; recognizes self-judgment may worsen symptoms). Shows some psychological mindedness (able to reflect on how brain is "trying to protect" her). Judgment appears intact. Cognitive functioning appears grossly intact despite reported concentration difficulties; memory seemed good for both recent events (detailed account of accident) and timeline. No evidence of dissociation during session. No psychotic symptoms observed. Denied current safety concerns. Client motivated for treatment, asking appropriate questions about diagnosis and treatment, willing to engage in recommended interventions.

ASSESSMENT:
Client presents 5 months post-MVA with symptoms consistent with trauma response. Symptoms span all PTSD criterion clusters (intrusive symptoms, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, alterations in arousal/reactivity), however diagnostic picture is complicated by several factors requiring ongoing assessment:

1. Symptom trajectory: Client reports symptoms have been improving over time (nightmares decreased from nightly to 1-2x/week, concussion symptoms resolved). This improvement trajectory is more consistent with acute stress response or adjustment disorder than chronic PTSD, though symptoms can wax and wane in PTSD as well.

2. Functional impairment: While client is experiencing distress and some functional impairment (avoiding certain activities, working from home more, seeing friends less), she maintains ability to work (performance apparently adequate), maintain marriage, and complete necessary activities of daily living including driving when needed. PTSD diagnosis requires "clinically significant distress or impairment" - client clearly has distress, but degree of impairment may not meet threshold for PTSD vs. adjustment disorder.

3. Pre-existing anxiety: Client reports baseline anxiety/"worrier" tendency prior to accident, though reports current symptoms are qualitatively different and more intense. Need to clarify: Is this an exacerbation of pre-existing anxiety disorder triggered by traumatic event, or new-onset trauma symptoms superimposed on baseline anxiety trait? This distinction has treatment implications.

4. Criterion A assessment: Client clearly experienced event involving actual or threatened death/serious injury and responded with fear (reports accident was "scary," "frightening"). This likely meets Criterion A for trauma exposure. However, client had relatively minor physical injuries and was not hospitalized, which may affect her perception of whether she "should" have ongoing symptoms (contributing to self-judgment).

5. Duration: Symptoms present for 5 months, which exceeds the 1-month minimum for PTSD but is still relatively recent. Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) requires symptoms 3 days to 1 month post-trauma; client is beyond that window. If symptoms continue without significant improvement, PTSD diagnosis would be appropriate.

Current diagnostic considerations (in order of likelihood):
1. F43.12 - Acute Stress Disorder (if considered symptoms from 1-5 months as prolonged acute stress response, though technically outside DSM-5 timeframe)
2. F43.10 - Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (Provisional) - symptoms present across clusters but may be subsyndromal; need standardized assessment
3. F43.22 - Adjustment Disorder with Anxiety - may better capture level of impairment and improving trajectory
4. F41.1 - Generalized Anxiety Disorder (Rule out) - need to clarify if pre-existing GAD being exacerbated by trauma vs. new trauma symptoms

Additional considerations:
- R/O Persistent Postconcussive Syndrome (ICD-10: F07.81) - some symptoms (concentration difficulties, irritability, sleep disturbance) can be attributed to concussion, though client reports physical symptoms mostly resolved
- No evidence of Major Depressive Disorder (some mood changes but no pervasive anhedonia, hopelessness, or neurovegetative symptoms)
- No evidence of Panic Disorder (anxiety is situational/trauma-related, not unexpected panic attacks)

STRENGTHS/PROTECTIVE FACTORS:
- Motivated for treatment, seeking help relatively early
- Good insight and self-awareness
- Maintains functioning in key life domains
- Supportive spouse
- Using some adaptive coping strategies
- Symptoms improving over time (suggesting natural recovery process may be occurring)
- No suicidal ideation
- Recognizes accident was not her fault (no inappropriate guilt)

RISK FACTORS/CLINICAL CONCERNS:
- Avoidance behaviors may become more entrenched if not addressed
- Self-judgment and minimization may prevent her from seeking appropriate support
- Pre-existing anxiety may complicate recovery
- Social isolation increasing (seeing friends less, not talking about experience)
- Relying on avoidance as primary coping mechanism

RISK ASSESSMENT:
Suicide risk: LOW - denies SI/HI, plan, intent; has supportive relationships and reasons for living
Violence risk: LOW - no history or current concerns
Overall safety: No immediate concerns
