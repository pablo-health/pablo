ASSESSMENT:
Client presents with complex clinical picture requiring comprehensive differential diagnosis. Primary diagnostic consideration is Bipolar Disorder (Type I vs. Type II to be determined based on severity/duration of elevated episodes and presence of psychotic features, which were not clearly established in initial interview). Client reports distinct periods of elevated/expansive mood with classic manic features including decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts/flight of ideas, pressured speech (by report), increase in goal-directed activity, grandiosity/inflated self-esteem, and excessive involvement in high-risk pleasurable activities (sexual indiscretions, spending sprees, substance use). These episodes are followed by major depressive episodes. Family history is significant for bipolar disorder (maternal aunt), suggesting genetic vulnerability. However, several complicating factors must be carefully evaluated before confirming diagnosis:

1) Substance-Induced Mood Disorder must be ruled out: Client uses cocaine regularly with increased use during elevated periods. Cocaine can induce manic-like symptoms. Marijuana use is daily. Timeline assessment needed to determine if mood symptoms occur independently of substance use or are substance-induced. Client reports elevated mood precedes substance use, but this requires careful longitudinal tracking.

2) Trauma-related symptoms: Client has experienced multiple traumatic assaults in context of sex work. Reports flashbacks, nightmares, and avoidance related to trauma. While mood symptoms appear primary, trauma may be exacerbating or complicating clinical picture. Differential includes PTSD or trauma-related mood dysregulation.

3) Borderline Personality Disorder features: Client demonstrates pattern of unstable intense relationships, affective instability, impulsivity, recurrent suicidal ideation, and history of self-harm. Identity disturbance evident ("I feel like two different people"). However, distinct episodic nature of mood symptoms and decreased need for sleep during elevated periods are more consistent with bipolar disorder than BPD alone. May have both.

4) Occupational factors: The nature of client's work (sex work) involves irregular schedule, nighttime hours, potential sleep disruption, substance use in work context, exposure to violence, and significant psychological stress, all of which could contribute to mood instability. Additionally, societal stigma and family rejection related to work are chronic stressors.

5) Cyclothymic Disorder vs. Bipolar II vs. Bipolar I: Duration and severity of elevated episodes not fully established. Need to determine if elevated episodes meet criteria for hypomanic episodes (4+ days) vs. manic episodes (7+ days or requiring hospitalization). Presence of psychotic features during elevated episodes was not assessed and would distinguish Bipolar I.

Immediate safety concerns: Passive suicidal ideation present but LOW acute risk based on denial of plan/intent, presence of protective factors (friends, does not want to cause harm like unit friend's suicide did to family), future orientation, and engagement with treatment. However, client describes increased SI during depressive episodes and has access to means (pills), requiring close monitoring and safety planning.

Functional impairment: Significant across multiple domains including occupational (canceling work during depressive episodes, dangerous decision-making during elevated episodes), financial (impulsive spending leading to debt), interpersonal (pattern of failed relationships, family estrangement, lost friendships), and personal safety (high-risk behaviors, exposure to violence).

Clinical summary: 27-year-old female with probable Bipolar Disorder (type to be determined) complicated by substance use, trauma history, occupational risk factors, and possible personality disorder features. Requires ongoing diagnostic clarification through mood tracking, detailed timeline analysis, and potential substance use reduction to clarify independent mood episodes. Genetic loading (family history) supports bipolar diagnosis. Client is motivated for treatment and demonstrates good insight.
